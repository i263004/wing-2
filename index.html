Deep Space & Cosmic Scale

Beyond the edge of our Solar System stretches an ocean of stars, stellar nurseries, and hundreds of billions of galaxies separated by distances so immense that light itself takes millennia—or eons—to cross them.

The Light-Year Time Machine

A light-year is the distance light travels in one Earth year (~9.46 trillion km). Because light travels at a finite speed (299,792 km/s), looking deeper into space is literally looking back in time.

Deep-Sky Observation Exhibits

3 Curated Artifacts
Exhibit 2.1 • Deep-Sky Survey

The Hubble Deep Field

In December 1995, astronomers pointed the Hubble Space Telescope at an apparently empty patch of sky in Ursa Major the size of a grain of sand held at arm's length. Over 10 consecutive days, it revealed nearly 3,000 previously unseen galaxies stretching back toward the dawn of time.

Exhibit 2.2 • Large-Scale Structure

The Cosmic Web & Filaments

On the grandest scales, galaxies are not scattered randomly. Dark matter's gravity weaves gas and galaxy clusters into a vast, sponge-like tapestry of glowing filaments separated by immense cosmic voids hundreds of millions of light-years wide.

Exhibit 2.3 • Relic Radiation

Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)

The oldest light in the universe, released just 380,000 years after the Big Bang when the cosmos cooled enough for atoms to form. Microscopic temperature ripples in this afterglow are the gravitational seeds of every galaxy we see today.

Room 8 • Stellar Nurseries

Stars & Nebulae

Inside towering clouds of interstellar hydrogen and cosmic dust, gravity ignites nuclear fusion—giving birth to blazing new suns and forging the heavy elements that make up planets and life.

Exhibit 2.4 • Eagle Nebula (M16)

Pillars of Creation

Located 6,500 light-years away, these towering columns of cool molecular hydrogen and dust stretch 4 to 5 light-years tall. Intense ultraviolet radiation from nearby newborn stars erodes their tips while dense knots inside collapse into protostars.

Exhibit 2.5 • Messier 42 (1,344 LY)

The Orion Nebula

Visible to the naked eye in Orion's sword, M42 is the closest massive star-forming region to Earth. Astronomers have imaged over 150 glowing protoplanetary disks ("proplyds") encircling infant stars—embryonic solar systems in the making.

Exhibit 2.6 • Supernova Remnant (M1)

The Crab Nebula & Pulsar

The expanding wreckage of a massive star whose cataclysmic supernova explosion was recorded by astronomers in 1054 CE. At its core spins a city-sized neutron star pulsar rotating 30 times every second, sweeping beams of radiation across space.

Room 9 • Cosmic Titans

Black Holes & Galaxies

From sprawling spiral islands of hundreds of billions of stars to gravitational singularities where spacetime curves infinitely, explore the most extreme structures in the universe.

Exhibit 2.7 • EHT Collaboration (2019)

First Black Hole Image (M87*)

Captured by synchronizing eight radio observatories across Earth into a planet-sized virtual telescope, this landmark image reveals the glowing plasma ring and dark shadow of a 6.5-billion-solar-mass supermassive black hole 55 million light-years away.

Exhibit 2.8 • Messier 31 (2.53M LY)

The Andromeda Galaxy (M31)

Home to roughly one trillion stars, Andromeda is the largest spiral galaxy in our Local Group. Hurtling toward us at 110 km/s, it will merge with the Milky Way in approximately 4.5 billion years to form a giant elliptical galaxy ("Milkomeda").

Exhibit 2.9 • Our Home Galaxy

The Milky Way & Sagittarius A*

Our barred spiral galaxy spans 100,000 light-years and harbors 100–400 billion stars. Anchoring its galactic center is Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*), a supermassive black hole weighing 4 million times the mass of our Sun.
